Call, text, or connect on WhatsApp. Blog How Runpod Serverless billing works when a job fails to complete Why your balance can drop even when nothing succeeds, how to find where the money went, and how to keep it from happening again. Updated October 16, 2026 Table of contents Share Get started Let's say you've been hard at work implementing a chatbot using Runpod serverless, and after an hour of testing you can see your balance chipping away a bit, even though you know you aren't using any storage and don't have any stopped pods, even though you haven't actually had a successful request served yet. That's a common surprise, and an understandable one. From the outside, a failed request looks like nothing happened. However, on the platform side, a GPU was allocated, a container started, a model loaded into memory, and your code ran. All of that is compute time, and it's worth a breakdown of where your spend is going. If you take one idea from this post, make it this one: Serverless billing follows worker time, not request outcomes. You pay for the seconds a worker is running on a GPU (starting up, executing, or waiting for more work), whether the jobs it touched succeeded, failed, or never started. Below, we walk through the billable lifecycle of a worker, the places where "failed" and "billed" overlap, how to trace a surprise charge back to its cause, and the safeguards that stop a bad deploy from turning into a bad bill. We finish with what to do if you think you've been charged incorrectly. Key takeaways A worker is billed per second from when it starts until it stops. That covers startup (the cold start), execution, and the idle window after each job. Active workers are billed for as long as they're enabled. A job's status ( COMPLETED , FAILED , TIMED_OUT ) tells you what happened to the work, not what you were charged. A failed job that held onto that GPU for ten minutes costs the same as a job that returned a successful result. The expensive failures are the ones that repeat: workers that crash during startup while jobs wait in the queue, handlers that hang until the execution timeout, and requests that get retried by the platform or by your client. Your maximum burn rate is Max workers * GPUs per worker * your GPU's rate, plus any active workers. You'll want to be diligent about what you set in thee variables. During your testing and iteration phases, you'll want to use as few workers and as cheap a GPU spec as you can technically get away with. To stop spend right away, set Max workers and Active workers to 0, then purge the queue. If you reconcile your usage and the numbers still don't add up, contact support with your endpoint IDs, a UTC time window, and job and worker IDs. 1. The billable lifecycle of a Serverless worker A Serverless endpoint is a queue (or, for load-balanced endpoints, a router) that sits in front of a pool of workers. Each worker is a container running your image on a GPU. The platform scales the pool up and down for you, between the Active workers floor and the Max workers ceiling you set. Flex workers start when there's work and shut down when the idle timeout expires. You pay only while they're running. Active workers run around the clock so requests never wait on a cold start. What a flex worker is billed for Billing is per second. Only the stages where your container is running on a GPU are billed. Stage What’s happening Billed? Queued, no worker available Your job waits in the queue for capacity No. No worker is running. Worker start (cold start) The container starts, your code imports, and models load into GPU memory Yes Execution Your handler processes the job Yes Idle The worker waits for another job until the idle timeout expires Yes Throttled The worker can’t run because the host machine is out of capacity No. Compute time only accrues while the worker is running. Stopped / exited The worker has shut down No Some details that matter in practice: Billing is per second. Short bursts cost little, which is the joy of Serverless. However, a small cost repeated thousands of times does not. Cold starts are real compute. If your container downloads a 20 GB model every time it starts, you pay for that download every time. FlashBoot (on by default, at no extra cost), cached models, network volumes, and baking weights into the image all make this shorter. The idle window restarts after every job. At the default 5-second idle timeout, a steady trickle of requests can keep a worker alive indefinitely. That's normally what you want. It stops being what you want when every one of those requests is failing. Load-balanced endpoints follow the same rule: you pay for the time workers are running. The difference is that there's no job queue. Requests go straight to healthy workers, so the health check your worker exposes decides whether it gets traffic. A worker that reports healthy while your app is broken will keep receiving requests and keep running. 2. Execution, success, retries, startup failures, and what your users see Most billing confusion comes from the different signals and how they relate to what you actually pay. Five signals, and the one that maps to your bill Each signal lives at its own layer. Only worker state tells you what you were charged. Layer Where you see it What it tells you How it relates to billing Worker state This is the bill Workers tab, /health Whether a container is running on a GPU: initializing, idle, running, throttled, unhealthy, or outdated This is the billing layer. Running time is billed. Job status Requests tab, /status/{job_id} Whether the platform considers the job finished: IN_QUEUE , IN_PROGRESS , COMPLETED , FAILED , CANCELLED , TIMED_OUT Describes how worker time was spent, not whether it was billed Handler result The job’s output Whether your code produced something useful Billing never sees it. COMPLETED only means your handler returned without signaling an error. Retries The retried count in metrics and /health , plus your client logs That the same work ran more than once Every attempt uses worker time User-visible result Your application What your end user experienced Can differ completely from all of the above hese combinations catch people out regularly: FAILED , and billed in full. Say your handler spends 9 minutes generating a video and then throws on the final encode. The job is FAILED , and you pay for 9 minutes of execution. COMPLETED , but useless. If your handler catches an exception and returns {"status": "error"} as ordinary output, the platform records a successful job. Return errors under the error key instead. They'll be counted as failures, and your dashboards will show what actually happened. Timed out at the client, still running on the GPU. A synchronous /runsync call returns after a set wait even when the job isn't done, and your HTTP client may give up even sooner. Neither one stops the job. It keeps running, and billing, until it finishes, hits the server-side timeout, or you cancel it. Retried, so billed again. If a worker dies mid-job (say, the process is killed for running out of memory), the platform can put the job back in the queue for another worker. That helps reliability, but if a request always crashes the worker, you pay for every attempt. Client-side retries add to the total: resubmitting a timed-out /run call creates a second job while the first may still be running. Never ready, still billed. A worker that spends 90 seconds loading and then crashes has used 90 seconds of GPU time. It never processed a job, so it won't appear in your request history. It shows up only in worker states, logs, and billing. 3. Where your balance goes when nothing seems to work Eight ways a balance drains while nothing succeeds Grouped by where the failure happens. Most are cheap once; they get expensive because they repeat automatically. Pattern What happens Why it bills Startup — before the worker takes a job Startup crash loops Hardest to spot A bad image, a missing environment variable, a CUDA mismatch, a failed model download, or running out of memory during model load makes the container exit before it takes a job. Jobs are still queued, so the autoscaler keeps starting workers, on this machine and others, up to Max workers. Each attempt runs on a GPU until it crashes. Ten workers that each spend two minutes loading a model and then fail cost 20 GPU-minutes per round, with nothing completed. Slow failures The worker fails only at the end of a long step: downloading weights from an external hub on every cold start, waiting on a dependency that never responds, or retrying a download in a loop. Cost grows with time-to-failure. A worker that fails in 3 seconds costs very little. One that fails after a 6-minute download costs a lot more. Execution — while a job is running Hung handlers The handler deadlocks, waits on an external API with no timeout, spins in a loop, or hits a GPU error and stops making progress. The job keeps the worker busy until the execution timeout ends it. With the timeout off or set very high, one stuck job can keep a worker running for hours. Fast failures at high volume Every request fails quickly, from a bad input schema, an expired downstream credential, or a model that only returns errors, and traffic keeps arriving. The steady flow of requests keeps resetting the idle timeout, and the autoscaler keeps capacity matched to the queue. You pay for a pool of workers whose only output is errors. Poison requests and retry amplification One input crashes the worker process (an out-of-memory kill, a segfault in a native library). The platform treats the lost job as retryable and sends it to another worker, which it crashes too. You pay for every attempt, plus the cold start of every replacement worker. Traffic and capacity — around the jobs Abandoned and duplicated work A user closes their browser, your client times out, or a retry wrapper resubmits a request, and nothing cancels the original job. The original runs to completion, or to its timeout, with nobody waiting. Every retry is another billed job. Always-on capacity that isn’t doing useful work Active workers stay on for a broken image, an endpoint whose traffic has moved elsewhere, or a test endpoint nobody cleaned up. Active workers bill for as long as they’re enabled. One forgotten active worker bills 24 hours a day. Unexpected traffic An API key leaks, a cron job misfires, or someone points a load test at production. The endpoint does its job, scaling up and serving requests, for traffic you didn’t mean to send. 4. How to trace a surprise charge Start at the bill and work toward the cause. First pin down where and when the money went, then work out why . Step 1: Find the spend in your billing data In the console, open Billing to see usage over time, broken down by resource. You want two things from it: which endpoint (or endpoints) account for the charge, and the time window where the rate changed. On a team, charges go to the team owner's account, and only members with the billing or admin role can see them. For more detail, pull serverless billing history from the REST API. Hourly buckets for a single endpoint usually make it obvious when an incident started: curl -s "https://rest.runpod.io/v1/billing/endpoints?endpointId=$ENDPOINT_ID&bucketSize=hour&startTime=2026-10-12T00:00:00Z&endTime=2026-10-13T00:00:00Z" \ -H "Authorization: Bearer $RUNPOD_API_KEY" If you prefer the command line, runpodctl billing returns the same data. Write down the window where spend jumped, in UTC. The rest of this section is about explaining that window. Step 2: Check endpoint health and metrics Open the endpoint, go to the Metrics tab, and filter to your window. Compare these: Completed, failed, and retried requests. A high share of failures, or retries that don't stop, point to the handler and poison-request patterns in section 3. Execution time. Spikes, or a flat line at your execution timeout, mean jobs are hanging or running slow. Cold start count and cold start time. Lots of cold starts with few completed jobs is what a startup crash loop looks like. It can also mean the idle timeout is too short for bursty traffic. Worker counts by state. Workers running while nothing completes is the clearest sign you're paying for failure. For a live snapshot, call the health endpoint: curl -s "https://api.runpod.ai/v2/$ENDPOINT_ID/health" \ -H "Authorization: Bearer $RUNPOD_API_KEY" { "jobs" : { "completed" : 12 , "failed" : 340 , "inProgress" : 10 , "inQueue" : 2210 , "retried" : 41 }, "workers" : { "idle" : 0 , "running" : 10 } } This response shows thousands of jobs queued, ten workers running, and nearly everything failing. When you see something like it, stop the endpoint first (section 7) and diagnose afterward. Step 3: Read the request history The Requests tab lists recent jobs with their status, delay time, and execution time. Look for: TIMED_OUT jobs, and how close their execution time is to your timeout. IN_PROGRESS jobs that are far older than your usual execution time. FAILED jobs with long execution times. These are your most expensive failures. Repeated, near-identical inputs, which usually mean client retries. For any single job, /status returns its state and its timings in milliseconds: curl -s "https://api.runpod.ai/v2/$ENDPOINT_ID/status/$JOB_ID" \ -H "Authorization: Bearer $RUNPOD_API_KEY" { "id" : "a1b2c3d4-..." , "status" : "FAILED" , "delayTime" : 84213 , "executionTime" : 600000 , "error" : "..." } Note that delay time doesn't measure billing. It's how long a job waited before a worker picked it up. Waiting in the queue with no worker costs nothing. If the delay came from a cold start, though, the worker that was starting up was billed for that time. Step 4: Look at worker behavior The Workers tab shows each worker's current state, and you can open its logs from there. Look for: Churn: lots of workers appearing and disappearing in a short time, particularly ones moving between initializing and unhealthy. Workers stuck in one state: initializing for much longer than your normal cold start, or running with no matching in-progress jobs. Outdated workers after a deploy. During a rolling update, old workers keep serving while new ones start, so for a while you pay for both. If the new release is broken, the new workers fail while the old ones drain away. Step 5: Read the logs Worker logs record your container's stdout and stderr from startup on. That makes them the best evidence for startup failures, which are the one category that never shows up in request history. Endpoint logs combine output from all workers. Things to search for: The last lines before a worker exits: tracebacks, CUDA error , out of memory , Killed , missing files, failed downloads. The gap between container start and your "ready" log line. That gap is your billed cold start. The same job ID in more than one worker's logs, which means the job was retried. Error messages from downstream services your handler calls. Logs help much more when your code writes them on purpose. Print a line when startup begins, after each slow step, and when the worker is ready for jobs, and put the job ID in every log line inside the handler. Use flush=True (or unbuffered output) so the last lines before a crash actually get written. Workers are short-lived, so if you need logs for the long term, send them to your own logging service too. Settings that shape your spend Every Serverless endpoint setting trades off latency, reliability, and cost. These are the ones that matter most when something goes wrong. Endpoint settings that shape your spend Each setting trades off latency, reliability, and cost. These matter most when something goes wrong. Setting What it controls Effect on spend during failures Recommendation Capacity — how much can run at once Max workers The most workers that can run at once Sets your blast radius. A crash loop or retry storm can only burn as many workers as you allow. Size it to real peak demand plus some headroom, not to “as high as it goes.” Active workers Workers kept running at all times Billed continuously, whether they’re healthy and busy or not. 0 for dev and staging. In production, only as many as your latency target needs. GPUs per worker How many GPUs each worker gets Multiplies the cost of every worker-second, failed ones included. Use the fewest your model needs. GPU type priority Which GPUs workers can land on, and in what order When your first choice is scarce, falling back to larger GPUs raises the per-second rate. List only GPU types you’re willing to pay for. Timeouts — how long anything can run Idle timeout How long a worker waits for new work after a job (default 5 s) Longer timeouts keep failing workers alive between requests and add a billed tail to every burst. Keep it short unless your cold starts are expensive and your traffic is bursty. Execution timeout Never disable The longest a job can run (default 600 s) Your most important cap on hung jobs. Never turn it off in production. Set it a little above your real p99 execution time, and override it per request when needed. Job TTL How long a job can exist before it expires Queued jobs are what make the autoscaler start workers. A long TTL keeps a broken endpoint trying to start workers for longer. Match it to how long the result is still useful to your caller. Scaling — how fast capacity grows Auto-scaling type and threshold When to add workers, based on queue delay or request count Aggressive scaling adds workers faster, and that includes during a failure. Use conservative thresholds for batch workloads that can tolerate a queue. Startup — how fast and how reliably workers boot FlashBoot Faster restarts for recently used workers Makes billed cold starts shorter. Leave it on. It’s on by default and costs nothing extra. Model caching / network volume Where model weights come from at startup Removes the repeated download from every cold start. Don’t pull weights from the internet on every start. CUDA version filter Which host drivers workers can run on Keeps workers off incompatible hosts where they’d fail at startup. Set it to the versions your image was built for. Per-request execution policies let you set tighter limits for specific workloads without touching the endpoint: { "input" : { "prompt" : "..." }, "policy" : { "executionTimeout" : 120000 , "ttl" : 600000 , "lowPriority" : true } } ‍ executionTimeout and ttl are in milliseconds. lowPriority jobs don't make the endpoint scale up, so they suit work that should only use capacity that's already running. Work out your maximum burn rate for every production endpoint: max hourly spend: (Max workers × GPUs per worker × flex hourly rate)+ (Active workers × GPUs per worker × active hourly rate) 6. Safeguards and alerts Before you deploy Test the image before it scales. Run your handler locally with a test input ( python handler.py --test_input '{"input": {...}}' ), or start the image on a single Pod to make sure it loads and runs on real hardware. Canary new releases. Deploy to a separate endpoint, or set Max workers to 1, send a few real requests, and check the logs before you scale up. Pin image tags. With :latest , it's hard to know what's running and easy to ship something untested. Bake in or cache your model. It makes cold starts faster and removes a common cause of startup failures. In your worker code Make failures fast, loud, and contained. Do the expensive setup once per worker, outside the handler. Check everything you can before loading the model, and return errors instead of crashing: import os import sys import runpod # 1. Fail fast on configuration problems, before any expensive work. REQUIRED_ENV = [ "MODEL_PATH" ] missing = [k for k in REQUIRED_ENV if not os.environ.get(k)] if missing: print(f "FATAL: missing environment variables: {missing}" , flush=True) sys.exit( 1 ) import torch if not torch.cuda.is_available(): print( "FATAL: no CUDA device visible" , flush=True) sys.exit( 1 ) print( "startup: loading model" , flush=True) model = load_model(os.environ[ "MODEL_PATH" ]) # once per worker, not per job print( "startup: ready" , flush=True) def handler(job): job_id = job[ "id" ] inp = job.get( "input" ) or {} # 2. Reject bad input in milliseconds, not minutes. if "prompt" not in inp: return { "error" : "input.prompt is required" } try : # 3. Put timeouts on anything that can block (HTTP calls, locks, subprocesses). result = model.generate(inp[ "prompt" ], max_tokens=inp.get( "max_tokens" , 512 )) return { "output" : result} except torch.cuda.OutOfMemoryError as e: print(f "[{job_id}] OOM: {e}" , flush=True) # 4. Fail this job once, and recycle the worker so the next job starts clean. return { "error" : f "out of memory: {e}" , "refresh_worker" : True} except Exception as e: print(f "[{job_id}] error: {e}" , flush=True) return { "error" : str(e)} runpod.serverless.start({ "handler" : handler}) How the pattern works: Returning a dictionary with an error key marks the job FAILED , so your metrics match what really happened. refresh_worker stops the worker after the current job. Use it when an error could leave the GPU or the process in a bad state. For deeper startup validation, the Runpod SDK supports fitness checks that run before a worker starts taking jobs. Use them to check GPU health, available memory, and required files. In your client Cancel what you abandon. When a caller gives up, call /cancel/{job_id} . Don't resubmit blindly. Check /status on the original job before you retry. Cap the number of retries and use exponential backoff. Stop sending when error rates spike. A simple circuit breaker in your client keeps fast failures from scaling into a large bill. On your account Watch your balance. Turn on low-balance notifications in billing settings. If you use automatic top-ups, choose a threshold and amount that match what you'd accept spending in a bad hour. Auto-pay also removes the hard stop you'd otherwise get when the balance hits zero. Scope your API keys. Use separate keys for production, staging, and one-off scripts, each with the narrowest permissions that work. Then a leaked key is easy to contain. Limit who can change endpoint settings. On teams, give billing visibility to the people who watch costs, and edit access only to the people who deploy. Automated monitoring and an emergency brake The console is good for investigating, but alerts need to reach you on their own. This script polls /health and alerts you when an endpoint is clearly paying for failure. With AUTO_BRAKE=1 , it also sets Max workers to 0. Run it on a schedule, from somewhere other than the endpoint it's watching. You can add a spend check in the same way: pull the last hour from the serverless billing history API, and brake if it's over your budget. 7. Containment runbook: stopping the spend If your balance is dropping and you can't explain why right away, stop the spend first and investigate second. A crash loop at full Max workers costs the same per minute whether or not anyone is watching. Take a quick snapshot. Spend 60 seconds screenshotting the Workers and Metrics tabs and saving the /health output. Worker logs are easiest to collect while the workers still exist. Set Max workers to 0 and Active workers to 0. You can do this in the endpoint's settings or through the API. This is the brake: no new workers start, and existing workers are scaled down. Jobs that are mid-run when their worker stops may not finish. Purge the queue. Queued jobs will start workers again as soon as you raise Max workers. If they're not worth running, clear them: curl -s -X POST "https://api.runpod.ai/v2/$ENDPOINT_ID/purge-queue" \ -H "Authorization: Bearer $RUNPOD_API_KEY" Cancel the stuck in-progress jobs you can identify: curl -s -X POST "https://api.runpod.ai/v2/$ENDPOINT_ID/cancel/$JOB_ID" \ -H "Authorization: Bearer $RUNPOD_API_KEY" If the traffic is unexpected, shut off its source. Pause whatever upstream job or queue is sending requests, and rotate the API key if there's any chance it leaked. Confirm the meter has stopped. Check that no workers are running, and that billing history shows no new usage on the endpoint over the following hour. Network volume storage keeps billing. Fix it, then reopen gradually. Deploy the fix, set Max workers to 1, send test requests, and watch the logs before you restore normal limits. Don't start by deleting the endpoint. Scaling to zero stops compute charges just as effectively, and keeping the endpoint preserves the metrics, request history, and logs that you and our support team will need. 8. Escalating a suspected billing defect Most surprise charges turn out to be one of the patterns in section 3: real compute spent on work that didn't succeed. Billing systems can have defects too, though. If you've reconciled your usage and the numbers still don't add up, we want to hear about it. Failures don't stop it. They only change what you get for that time. Every expensive pattern above has the same shape, something repeating automatically, and each one has a limit you control: Max workers caps the blast radius. The execution timeout caps hung jobs. The job TTL and a queue purge cap how long the autoscaler keeps trying. Fail-fast startup code caps what each crash costs.
